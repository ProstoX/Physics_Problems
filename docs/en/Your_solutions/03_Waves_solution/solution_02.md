## 2. String Harmonics

A guitar string is 64 cm long and has a fundamental frequency (one antinode) of 330 Hz. What is the speed of the wave on this string?

---

### **Variables and Units**

Before calculating the solution, here is a breakdown of the symbols used for this specific problem:

* **$L$ (Length):** The physical length of the vibrating string. Measured in **meters (m)**. (Note: Centimeters must be converted to meters for standard wave calculations).
* **$f_1$ (Fundamental Frequency):** The lowest frequency at which the string resonates, forming exactly one antinode (the point of maximum vibration). Measured in **Hertz (Hz)**, which is equivalent to cycles per second ($1/\text{s}$).
* **$\lambda$ (Lambda):** The wavelength of the wave. Measured in **meters (m)**.
* **$v$ (Velocity/Speed):** The speed at which the wave propagates along the string. Measured in **meters per second (m/s)**.

---

### **Step-by-Step Solution**

**Step 1: Convert units to standard standard SI units**
The length of the string is given in centimeters. Convert this to meters to ensure the final wave speed is in standard m/s:
$$L = 64 \text{ cm} = 0.64 \text{ m}$$

**Step 2: Determine the wavelength ($\lambda$)**
A fundamental frequency (also called the first harmonic) creates exactly one antinode on a string fixed at both ends. This means the length of the string ($L$) represents exactly half of a full wavelength ($\lambda/2$).
$$L = \frac{\lambda}{2}$$

Rearrange the formula to solve for the wavelength ($\lambda$):
$$\lambda = 2L$$
$$\lambda = 2 \cdot 0.64$$
$$\lambda = 1.28 \text{ m}$$

**Step 3: Calculate the speed of the wave ($v$)**
Now that we have both the frequency ($f_1 = 330 \text{ Hz}$) and the wavelength ($\lambda = 1.28 \text{ m}$), we can use the standard wave equation to find the speed:
$$v = f \cdot \lambda$$
$$v = 330 \cdot 1.28$$
$$v = 422.4 \text{ m/s}$$

The speed of the wave on this guitar string is **422.4 m/s**.
